7  flagpoles, and spinors as points on a projective line

this page is under construction

The last two chapters kept running into the same two facts: a spinor \mathbf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} doesn’t care about an overall phase, and it doesn’t care about an overall (nonzero) scale. I want to take those two facts seriously as a definition rather than as a pair of annoying caveats, because doing so explains, in one stroke, both why a spinor can be drawn as a point on a sphere and why that sphere has the angle-doubling property baked in from the start.

7.1 throwing away two of the four real numbers

\alpha and \beta are each complex, so naively a spinor needs four real numbers. But since \mathbf J and c\mathbf J (any nonzero complex c) are the same spinor, I’m free to pick c = 1/\alpha and normalize the top entry to exactly 1:

\mathbf{J} = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} \;\sim\; \begin{pmatrix}1\\ \beta/\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

Everything that matters about the spinor is contained in the single complex number \beta/\alpha — two real numbers, not four. That’s exactly enough to specify a point on a sphere (longitude and latitude), which is why the Poincaré/Bloch sphere picture works at all: it isn’t a visualization trick bolted on afterward, it’s a direct consequence of quotienting by an overall complex factor.

7.2 putting the two discarded numbers back as a flagpole

It still feels wasteful to throw \arg(\alpha) and |\alpha| away completely, so eigenchris’ picture keeps them visible: draw the spinor not as a bare point on the sphere, but as a flagpole planted at that point. The length of the pole encodes the overall scale we’re ignoring, and the angle of a little flag at the top encodes the overall phase we’re ignoring. Changing either one moves the flag or stretches the pole, but the point where the pole meets the sphere — the only part that is physically meaningful — never moves. I like this picture because it keeps the discarded information in view instead of pretending it never existed; it’s a visual reminder that “spinor” is a specific quotient, not a mysterious new kind of number.

7.3 real projective line first, because it’s easier to draw

To see why this quotient construction forces the angle-doubling, it helps to step down to real numbers first. Take the xy-plane, put a screen at x=1, and project every point (x,y) along the ray through the origin to wherever that ray crosses the screen, landing at (1, y/x). The destination depends only on the ratio y/x — so scaling (x,y) by any nonzero real number doesn’t move the projected point. This quotient space is the real projective line, \mathbb{RP}^1.

Two features of this projection matter a lot:

  • Points with x=0 never hit the screen — they get sent to a single point at infinity, which closes the line up into a loop.
  • Two points on opposite sides of a circle centered at the origin — i.e. related by an overall factor of -1 — land on the same point of the screen, since y/x is unchanged by flipping the sign of both coordinates.

That second bullet is the whole mechanism. Walk a point halfway around the circle and its projection sweeps across the entire screen (through infinity and back); walk it the rest of the way around and the projection repeats the same sweep. One trip around the circle is two trips around the projective line. This is the angle-doubling, with all the physics stripped away — it’s pure projective geometry.

7.4 the complex version is the spinor sphere

Doing the identical construction with a pair of complex numbers (\alpha,\beta), projecting via the ratio \beta/\alpha, gives the complex projective line, \mathbb{CP}^1. It can’t be drawn as literally as the real case (it would need four real dimensions), but the quotient is visualized the same way physicists already draw it: as the Riemann sphere, with \alpha=0 sent to the north pole (“point at infinity”) and antipodal-under-scaling points identified. The Poincaré sphere and the Bloch sphere are the same object wearing different hats — both are \mathbb{CP}^1, decorated with whichever six physical states (polarizations, or spin states) happen to sit at convenient locations on it.

So the cleanest definition I can give myself, after four chapters of examples, is: a spinor is a point on \mathbb{CP}^1, i.e. an equivalence class of nonzero pairs of complex numbers under overall complex scaling. Every angle-doubling fact we ran into — in optics, in Stern-Gerlach, and in the abstract SU(2) \to SO(3) covering map — is a restatement of the fact that this quotient turns one full loop into two. The next step up the staircase is to stop treating that factor of two as special to low dimensions and instead build it out of Clifford algebras, where it will appear for free in any dimension.
