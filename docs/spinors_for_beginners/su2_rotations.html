5  rotating Jones vectors: U(2), SU(2), and the Poincaré sphere

this page is under construction

Having written polarization states as Jones vectors in the previous chapter, the natural next question is: what matrices are allowed to act on them? Physically, a wave plate rotates one polarization into another by delaying the vertical component relative to the horizontal one by some phase. Mathematically, I want to find the group of matrices that can represent all physically realizable polarization changes, and understand why it has to be SU(2) rather than something bigger or smaller.

5.1 borrowing the logic from real rotations

Before touching complex vectors, it’s worth re-deriving the familiar case: what matrices R preserve the length of a real 3D vector v? Length-squared is v^\top v, and requiring (Rv)^\top(Rv) = v^\top v for every v forces

R^\top R = I.

These are the orthogonal matrices, O(3). Taking determinants of both sides gives (\det R)^2 = 1, so \det R = \pm 1: reflections get a -1, rotations get a +1. Restricting to \det R = +1 is what “special” means, and that’s SO(3).

5.2 the same logic, with complex entries

A Jones vector has complex entries, so “length” has to mean \mathbf{J}^\dagger\mathbf{J} (Hermitian conjugate, not plain transpose) or I’d get a complex number out of a thing that’s supposed to be a probability-like intensity. Demanding that a matrix U preserve this quantity for every \mathbf{J} gives, by the identical argument as above,

U^\dagger U = I,

the defining property of a unitary matrix. The 2\times2 unitary matrices form the group U(2). Taking the determinant of U^\dagger U = I gives |\det U|^2 = 1, so \det U is not forced to be \pm1 this time — it can be any unit-modulus complex number e^{i\theta}.

This is the first place where the complex case genuinely differs from the real one, and it matters: a generic U(2) matrix carries an unavoidable overall phase in its determinant. But I already know from the previous chapter that overall phase is physically invisible for Jones vectors. So U(2) has redundancy built into it — many different U(2) matrices produce the same physical rotation of the polarization ellipse, differing only by a phase that gets thrown away anyway.

5.3 picking a unique representative: SU(2)

To remove the redundancy, I can always rescale any U \in U(2) by e^{-i\theta/2} (where \theta = \arg\det U) to force its determinant to be exactly 1, without changing the physical transformation it represents. The matrices with

U^\dagger U = I, \qquad \det U = 1

form SU(2), and this is the group that acts on Jones vectors (and, as the next chapter shows, on quantum spin states) without any leftover ambiguity beyond the one we already knew about and chose to ignore.

5.4 the angle-doubling, finally made precise

Here’s the calculation that I think earns the name “spinor.” Physically, H and V polarizations are 90° apart in real space. But a half-wave plate — the device that swaps H \leftrightarrow V — corresponds to a full 180° rotation of the Jones vector on the Poincaré sphere (it has to go H \to D \to V, a half-circle). Chasing this through:

\text{physical angle } \theta \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{Poincaré-sphere angle } 2\theta.

This is why the SU(2) matrices that implement wave-plate rotations are conventionally written with half-angles:

U(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\theta/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\theta/2} \end{pmatrix}

for rotation about the H/V axis, and structurally similar matrices (built from Pauli matrices, which will show up again when we get to Lie algebras) for rotations about the other two axes of the sphere.

The thing I want to hold onto from this chapter is where the factor of two actually comes from. It isn’t some mysterious property bolted onto spinors by fiat — it falls straight out of the fact that (-1)\cdot\mathbf{J} and \mathbf{J} are the same physical state. A rotation that looks like “half a turn” on the sphere of physical polarization ellipses is a “full turn” on the sphere of Jones-vector directions, because the latter sphere has already glued antipodal points together. Once you see the gluing, the factor of two stops being surprising.
